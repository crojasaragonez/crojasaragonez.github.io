A government agency needs technology your company can build. The problem is real, the potential contract is large, and winning could open the door to more work.

It is easy to see the attraction. It is harder to know how much of your company you will have to commit before any of that revenue arrives.

For a technology supplier, selling to the government starts with a business decision: how long can we pursue this customer, what will it cost us, and what else could we be doing with the same people and money?

I keep coming back to a case described in [MIT Sloan's account of Tina Srivastava's research](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/why-u-s-government-struggling-open-innovation). A startup had technology the U.S. Air Force needed for national security. Getting the contract started took three years. At one point, the contracting officer stopped working with the company until it hired a lawyer.

Three years is a significant part of a young technology company's life. Salaries continue, products change, and other customers make purchasing decisions while that opportunity remains open.

## The sale starts costing money before it becomes a contract

Imagine a small software company considering a public-sector opportunity. Someone has to understand the tender, prepare the proposal, gather documentation, answer questions, and work through the technical and contractual requirements. A founder may become the salesperson, proposal writer, and coordinator for legal review.

That is time the company could spend improving its product or serving another customer. Some preparation may be reusable across bids. Work tailored to one agency may have little value if the deal disappears.

Then there is the uncertainty. Can the team plan around a decision date? Can it keep engineers available without a signed commitment? If the award moves, does the company have enough cash to keep waiting?

**The contract value tells you what you might sell. It tells you much less about what it will cost to become the supplier.**

These concerns appear in the evidence. The U.S. Government Accountability Office found that small businesses receiving Department of Defense contracts fell from **42,723 in 2011 to 24,296 in 2020**, a decline of 43%. Executives described unpredictable delays in award decisions, security clearances, work start dates, and payments. Many preferred the flexibility of private customers. [GAO's 2021 report](https://www.gao.gov/assets/d22104621.pdf) documents both the decline and those concerns, although it does not attribute the entire decline to bureaucracy.

Those examples concern U.S. defense procurement. They offer a useful way to examine a particular opportunity elsewhere, provided we also look at that buyer's actual requirements and payment process.

## Fewer competitors can look attractive, until you ask why

If other suppliers are staying away, the opportunity might seem even better. Less competition could mean a better chance of winning and more room to negotiate.

But a small field can also mean that other companies have looked at the cost of participating and decided to spend their time elsewhere. Before treating their absence as an advantage, I would want to understand it.

At the market level, fewer alternatives can leave buyers dependent on established suppliers. The Defense Department's [2022 competition report](https://media.defense.gov/2022/Feb/15/2002939087/-1/-1/1/STATE-OF-COMPETITION-WITHIN-THE-DEFENSE-INDUSTRIAL-BASE.PDF) described consolidation from 51 to five aerospace and defense prime contractors since the 1990s. In a separate [2019 review of TransDigm parts](https://www.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/documents/reports/2019-04/DODIG-2019-060.pdf), the inspector general identified excess profit on 46 of 47 parts examined, with reported profit percentages reaching 4,451%. That review used a 15% benchmark for reasonable profit; its findings concern the audited sample.

Those numbers do not tell a software company what it can expect to earn on a government project. They illustrate the pricing problems that can develop when a buyer has limited alternatives.

My interpretation is that a cycle can follow: difficult entry discourages suppliers, weaker competition increases the risk of inflated prices, and abuses prompt additional controls. A newcomer then has to absorb the cost of rules introduced in response to someone else's behavior.

For the supplier, understanding that cycle matters before submitting a bid. The customer may need your technology while operating a process that makes buying it difficult.

## Winning can create a different problem

Suppose the company wins. It still has to deliver, support the work, and get paid according to the contract. The cash required along the way deserves as much attention as the expected profit.

A project can look profitable on paper while requiring more cash than the business can comfortably commit. If the team must grow before a payment milestone, someone has to fund that gap. If acceptance takes longer than expected, the company needs room to absorb it.

There is also a product decision hidden inside the sale. A large customer may request integrations, deployment arrangements, and features that fit its own environment. Some of that work could make the product stronger for other customers. Some could leave the team maintaining a separate version for one institution.

I would want to know which kind of work we were accepting. The revenue may justify a dedicated team and a different operating model. But that choice should be deliberate, especially if one contract would consume most of the company's capacity.

## There are reasons to say yes

A government customer can offer work at a scale that is hard to find elsewhere. The problem may closely match the product. A supplier might already have the delivery capacity, the relevant experience, and enough cash to handle the purchasing cycle. The public value of the work can also matter to the people building it.

The purchasing route makes a difference too. The Defense Innovation Unit uses flexible acquisition tools to prototype commercial technology. Its [FY2022 review](https://www.diu.mil/fy22-year-in-review) reported an average of 142 days from solicitation to prototype contract award, against a target of 60 to 90 days.

Its [FY2023 annual report](https://www.diu.mil/2024-annual-report-files/) reported that 87% of contract award recipients were nontraditional vendors and 38% were first-time Defense Department vendors. Those are DIU's metrics, rather than figures for government procurement as a whole, but they show that newcomers do participate through these routes.

A supplier should still examine what follows a prototype. A paid pilot can be worthwhile on its own. If its economics depend on a much larger order later, the path to that order needs more support than an enthusiastic conversation.

## What would make this opportunity worth pursuing?

Before committing the team, I would put a few questions beside the potential contract value:

1. **How much can we afford to spend before an award?** Include proposal work, specialist advice, demonstrations, and the time of people who could be serving other customers.
2. **Can we finance delivery through the payment milestones?** Look at the actual acceptance and payment terms, and what a delay would mean for cash.
3. **Does the work fit the company we want to build?** Consider whether the requested development will serve other customers or require a lasting commitment to one buyer.
4. **What happens if the opportunity stalls?** Decide how much time and money to commit before reassessing it, and whether the rest of the business can continue without the award.

The answers could make a government contract a sensible next step for one supplier and an expensive distraction for another. Even the same company might reach a different decision a year later, with a stronger cash position or a more mature product.

I find the opportunity cost particularly difficult to judge. A large contract is visible: there is a number to put in a spreadsheet. The smaller customers you might have won, the product improvements you postponed, and the attention you spent waiting are much harder to count.

**Once we account for the cost of competing, the wait to get paid, and the work we would turn down, is the government still a customer worth pursuing?**
